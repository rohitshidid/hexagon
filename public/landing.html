Ten people, one island, no rules lawyer.

Hexagon is a Catan-like board game that runs in the browser for 2 to 10 players — over your Wi-Fi, through a tunnel, or on a free Render instance. The server owns every rule, so nobody miscounts the longest road and nobody can see your hand. Short a few players? Fill the empty seats with bots.

The problem

Board game night has a logistics problem.

The box seats four. Six people showed up. The expansion is at someone else's flat, two of you are dialling in from another city, and the one person who owns the rulebook has strong opinions about whether that road counts.

🪑

Everyone fits

Two players or ten, in the same room. The island grows to match the table — 19 hexes up to four players, 37 up to seven, 61 beyond that — so the tenth person still has somewhere to build rather than a corner nobody wanted.

⚖️

Nobody argues the rules

Longest Road is recomputed after every road and every settlement that might cut one. Largest Army counts itself. The distance rule is checked before your settlement lands. There is nothing left to disagree about.

🤖

Two people is enough

A two-player game of anything Catan-shaped is a bad game. Add bots in the lobby until the board feels contested — they place their own setup, roll, build, move the robber and answer your trade offers.

Authoritative server

The browser asks. The server decides.

Every rule lives in one file on the server. The client is a renderer with buttons — it cannot grant itself a resource, and it is never sent a card it shouldn't see.

Validation

Fifteen actions, each one refused by name.

Everything a player can do goes through a single act() entry point, which checks whose turn it is, which phase the game is in, and whether the move is legal — then mutates state. A rejected move comes back with the actual reason, which is also why the game is pleasant to learn: it tells you what you got wrong.

Because validation happens in one place, a hacked client is not a category of bug. The worst it can do is send a message the server says no to.

server → client
// a client that has been tampered with, asking nicely
→ action buildSettlement { vertexId: 41 }
← error "too close to another building"

→ action buildCity { vertexId: 12 }
← error "upgrade your own settlement"

→ action rollDice
← error "not your turn"

→ action bankTrade { give: "ore", get: "wheat" }
← error "need 4 ore"

// and one that plays by the rules
→ action buildRoad { edgeId: 33 }
← ok — 1 wood + 1 brick spent, longest road recomputed
Private hands

Your cards are yours, per socket.

The server does not broadcast one shared world. It builds a separate view for every player — you get your own resource counts and your own development cards; everyone else is a card count and nothing more. The robber steals at random from a hand the thief never gets to look at.

Which means "open dev tools and read the opponent's hand" doesn't work, because the opponent's hand was never sent to your machine.

rollDice

Two dice, then production on every matching hex the robber isn't sitting on.

buildRoad

Must connect to your network — and an opponent's settlement breaks the chain.

buildSettlement

Distance rule, road connection, and a piece limit of five.

buildCity

Upgrades your own settlement only. Four per player.

buyDev

Drawn from a shuffled deck scaled to the size of the board.

playDev

Knight, road building, year of plenty, monopoly. VP cards stay hidden.

bankTrade

4:1, or 3:1 / 2:1 if you settled a matching harbour.

proposeTrade

An offer to the table; anyone may accept, you choose who.

respondTrade

Accept or reject. Bots answer too, on their own valuation.

moveRobber

Any hex but the one it's on, then steal from an adjacent player.

discard

Exactly half, rounded down, from every hand of eight or more.

endTurn

Passes the turn and re-checks the win condition.

The game

If you have played Catan, you already know this.

Place two settlements and two roads in snake order. Roll, collect, build, trade, end turn. First to the target score wins. The differences are all about scale.

Costs & points

Four things to buy, and what they're worth.

Roads are cheap and only matter in aggregate — five connected segments takes Longest Road and its two points off whoever had it. Cities are the real engine: they pay double on every roll, which is why an ore-and-wheat corner is worth fighting over.

Games run to 10 points up to six players, and 12 above that so a bigger table doesn't end before everyone has had a turn. The host can push it to 14.

Board generation

The island is sized to the table.

The board is a hex of hexes with a radius picked from the player count. Resources are dealt from a bag weighted 4 : 4 : 4 : 3 : 3 — the same ratio as the printed board — and number tokens come from a 2-to-12 bag with no sevens, repeated as many times as the island needs.

Vertices and edges are derived by deduplicating hex corners, so adjacency is a fact of the geometry rather than a hand-written table. Ports land on coastal edges, sorted by angle around the island so they end up spaced rather than clumped.

Bots

They fill seats. They are not trying to humiliate you.

Press + Bot in the lobby, pick easy, normal or hard, and start. Bots place their own opening settlements, roll on cue, discard on a seven, drop the robber on whichever hex hurts the leader most, and bank-trade toward whatever they're short of. They evaluate your trade offers and sometimes say yes.

The strategy is deliberately greedy — city first, then settlement, then expand, then dev cards. Every bot action is stepped through one move at a time on a timer, so you watch the turn happen instead of the board teleporting.

Staying connected

A dropped Wi-Fi signal is not a lost game.

Someone will refresh the tab. Someone's laptop will sleep mid-turn. Neither should end an hour of play for the other nine people.

↩︎

Refresh puts you back

Your player id is kept in the browser. Reload and the socket reconnects, reattaches to the same seat, and your pieces, hand and dev cards are exactly where you left them.

⏳

The room waits ten minutes

A room isn't deleted the moment the last socket closes — that would make a refresh fatal. It's held for ten minutes after everyone has gone, then cleaned up.

🔐

A four-letter code, not an account

No sign-up, no email, no password. The host creates a game, gets a code like 7KQD, and shares it with the people they want in the room.

📶

Same Wi-Fi, no setup

The server prints a LAN URL on start. Everyone on the router opens it and joins — there is nothing to install on anyone else's machine.

🌍

Remote friends via a tunnel

Point cloudflared or ngrok at port 3000 and share the https URL. The client notices and switches to a secure wss:// socket on its own.

☁️

Or a permanent URL

render.yaml in the repo is a full Blueprint. Point Render at the repo, hit apply, and you have a URL that doesn't need your laptop to be awake.

Getting started

Two commands and a URL to share.

Node 18 or newer, two dependencies, no build step and nothing to compile. Clone it and you are hosting in under a minute.

1

Clone and install

Express for the static files and ws for the sockets. That is the entire dependency tree.

$ git clone https://github.com/rohitshidid/hexagon.git
$ cd hexagon
$ npm install
2

Start the server

It prints a local URL for you and a network URL for everyone else on the Wi-Fi.

$ npm start

  🎲  HEXAGON server running

  Local:   http://localhost:3000
  Network: http://192.168.1.146:3000
           ↑ share this one
3

Create a room

Open /play, enter a name, create a game and read out the four letters.

$ open http://localhost:3000/play

  → enter a name, hit Create Game
  → room code: 7KQD
  → short on people? hit + Bot
  → then Start Game
Playing with people elsewhere

The network URL is private. A tunnel isn't.

That 192.168.x.x address only resolves for people behind the same router. For anyone further away, expose the port you are already running — no deploy, no config, and it stops the moment you close the terminal.

A tunnel is open to anyone holding the link while it runs, and the room code is the only gate. Share it privately, and stop the tunnel when the game ends.

a second terminal
# free, no signup
$ brew install cloudflared
$ cloudflared tunnel --url http://localhost:3000

  https://plain-otter-fern.trycloudflare.com

# or, if you already have ngrok
$ ngrok http 3000

# or deploy once and stop thinking about it —
# render.yaml is a complete Blueprint
$ git push

Honestly

Five things it is not.

Knowing where something stops is more useful than a longer feature list. Here is exactly where Hexagon does.

✕

It is not affiliated with Catan.

This is an original implementation of a game shaped like one you know, written from scratch. It is not endorsed by, connected to, or licensed from Catan GmbH.

✕

It has no database.

Every room lives in a Map in one Node process. Restart the server and the games are gone — which also means it must run as exactly one instance, and never behind a load balancer without moving that state somewhere shared first.

✕

Player trading is one round deep.

You put an offer on the table and people accept or decline it. There is no counter-offer, no haggling protocol — if you want to negotiate, negotiate out loud and then post the deal you agreed on.

✕

The bots are not a tournament AI.

They play a greedy heuristic and they play it quickly. They will make a table of two humans feel like a table of six, and they will lose to anyone paying attention. A game of nothing but bots occasionally plateaus.

✕

Your seat lives in one browser.

Reconnection is keyed to a session stored locally. Clear your site data or move to a different browser mid-game and you will need to join again by name.

✕

A free instance falls asleep.

On Render's free plan the service sleeps after 15 idle minutes and any game in progress goes with it. An active game keeps it awake — the timer only starts once everybody has left.

Get everyone on the island.

Open source, two dependencies, and small enough to read on a train. Host it on the laptop you already have open.
